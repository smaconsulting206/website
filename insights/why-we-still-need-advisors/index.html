Why We Still Need Advisors: From Expert Authority to Critical Friend

Companion to The Uncommon Advisor, S1 E1

Dr Stephen Anthony, Avneet Singh Riat, Meehir Patel

There is a growing narrative in business today that feels both compelling and, at times, slightly misleading.

We have more access to information than ever before. We can analyse markets in minutes, generate strategy frameworks on demand, and tap into global best practice without leaving our desks. In that world, it is easy to ask whether the traditional role of the advisor or consultant is becoming obsolete.

But beneath that question sits a deeper misunderstanding—not about what advisors know, but about what they do. Because the real value of an advisor has never been just about providing answers. It has always been about helping organisations make sense of complexity, navigate uncertainty, and ultimately make better decisions.

And in many ways, that role has never been more important.

The Early Model: The Expert With the Answer

For much of its history, consulting was built on a relatively simple premise.

Organisations faced problems they either could not solve or could not align around internally. The consultant was brought in as an expert—someone who could diagnose the issue, recommend a solution, and provide the authority needed to move forward.

Stephen Anthony reflects on this dynamic with clarity. In many early engagements, organisations already understood the problem but needed an external voice to validate it. They needed, as he puts it, “somebody from the outside to say what we’re saying already.” The consultant, in that sense, was both expert and enabler of action.

This model worked well in more stable environments where problems were relatively contained and solutions could often be replicated. Success in one organisation could be translated to another. The idea of developing a playbook—and applying it repeatedly—was both efficient and commercially effective. But that world has changed.

The Collapse of “Copy and Paste” Consulting

One of the most significant shifts over the past two decades has been the gradual disappearance of standardised solutions.

Stephen describes a time when you could “fix a problem in one company and copy and paste that solution into the next.” That approach is now largely obsolete.

The modern organisation is shaped by a far more complex set of variables: technology ecosystems, organisational culture, leadership dynamics, regulatory pressures, and rapidly shifting markets. Even companies operating in the same sector can have fundamentally different starting points and constraints. What this means in practice is that context has become everything.

Meehir Patel reflects on this shift when he challenges the idea of a “one-size-fits-all” approach to consulting. In today’s environment, solutions cannot simply be imposed—they must be developed in collaboration with the organisation, shaped by its specific realities. This is not just a methodological change. It represents a philosophical shift in what it means to be an advisor.

From Authority to “Critical Friend”

If there is one idea that captures this transformation, it is the notion of the advisor as a critical friend. Stephen uses this phrase deliberately. The modern advisor is no longer defined solely by technical expertise or distance from the organisation. Instead, they are embedded in a relationship that requires both support and challenge. A critical friend is not there to agree. Nor are they there simply to instruct. They are there to ask difficult questions, to surface uncomfortable truths, and to work alongside leaders in navigating issues that do not have straightforward answers.

This shift reflects a deeper reality: organisations today are rarely lacking answers—they are struggling to determine which answers matter, and how to act on them.

Avneet Singh Riat reinforces this through his own experience across consulting and corporate environments. The most valuable advisory work, he suggests, is not about presenting solutions, but about helping organisations define outcomes, test assumptions, and navigate the path toward those outcomes with clarity and intent. In this sense, the advisor becomes less of a supplier and more of a partner.

Expertise Is Now the Entry Point, Not the Differentiator

One of the more subtle but important consequences of this evolution is the changing role of expertise itself. There was a time when technical capability was the primary differentiator in consulting. Today, that is no longer the case.

As Stephen puts it, arriving at the table and claiming to be technically better is not enough—“the technical skills are a given.” This raises an important question: if expertise is expected, what defines a great advisor? The answer lies in how that expertise is used. It lies in the ability to understand organisational context, to engage with stakeholders at multiple levels, and to apply knowledge in a way that is both relevant and actionable.

Meehir brings this into sharper focus through his work on human-centred leadership. Many of the challenges organisations face are not technical in nature—they are behavioural. They involve mindset, alignment, communication, and trust. A technically perfect solution that fails to land culturally is not a solution at all. This is why the role of the advisor has become more human, not less.

The Power of Perspective

Another enduring element of advisory work is the value of perspective. Over time, advisors build a breadth of experience by working across industries, organisations, and challenges. This exposure allows them to see patterns and connections that may not be immediately visible from within a single organisation. Stephen highlights this when he reflects on the diversity of sectors he has worked in over his career, noting how difficult it has become to find industries he has not touched.

But the real value of that experience is not just breadth—it is transferable insight. Advisors are able to recognise familiar dynamics in unfamiliar settings. They can draw on past experience without assuming that the same solution will apply. They can ask questions that challenge established thinking, simply because they are not embedded within it. Avneet builds on this by emphasising the importance of independence. Being outside the organisation allows advisors to identify blind spots and challenge assumptions without being constrained by internal politics or historical context. This perspective is often the catalyst for meaningful change.

Relationships Over Transactions

As advisory work has evolved, so too has the nature of the client relationship. The traditional model often treated consulting as a transactional engagement—deliver a piece of work, present the findings, and move on. But in a world where problems are more complex and solutions more nuanced, that model is increasingly limited.

Stephen reflects on the importance of relationships built over time. These are not short-term interactions but evolving partnerships, where trust and understanding deepen across multiple engagements. Meehir and Avneet both reinforce this idea through their emphasis on shared values. The most effective advisory work happens when there is alignment not just on objectives, but on how those objectives are pursued.

This introduces an important shift in mindset. Advisors are no longer simply chosen based on capability. They are chosen based on compatibility—on whether there is a shared approach to problem-solving, decision-making, and value creation. That alignment determines the success of the engagement as much as any technical input.

Mobilising, Not Replacing

One of the most defining characteristics of modern advisory is the move away from doing the work for organisations and toward enabling organisations to do the work themselves. Meehir captures this succinctly: “we mobilise you, we don’t replace you.” This principle reflects a deliberate shift away from dependency. Stephen illustrates this through long-term engagements where the nature of his role has evolved over time. Early involvement may be hands-on and intensive, but as the organisation’s capability grows, the advisor steps back. The relationship changes from operational support to strategic guidance—from doing to challenging and supporting.

Avneet reinforces this distinction, emphasising that the advisor’s role is to help define what should be done, rather than to execute it indefinitely. This approach does not always align with traditional commercial models. It requires advisors to balance financial incentives with a genuine commitment to client success. But when done well, it creates organisations that are stronger, more capable, and more resilient. And ultimately, it builds trust—the foundation of any long-term advisory relationship.

Helping Organisations Navigate Themselves

A particularly honest insight that emerges from the discussion is the idea that advisory work is often about helping organisations navigate challenges of their own making. Meehir touches on this directly, noting that in some cases, organisations engage consultants to solve problems that have developed internally over time. This is not a criticism—it is a reflection of how organisations operate.

Complex systems, competing priorities, and evolving strategies inevitably create misalignment. Over time, that misalignment can become difficult to address from within. This is where the advisor plays a crucial role. Not as a fixer from the outside, but as a facilitator of clarity—someone who can help the organisation step back, reassess, and move forward with intention.

As Stephen notes, the most successful engagements are those where the organisation genuinely wants to improve—where there is buy-in and openness to change. In those environments, the advisor’s impact is amplified.

The Future of Advisory Is More Human

Looking ahead, it is clear that the role of the advisor will continue to evolve. Technology will continue to reshape how information is accessed and how analysis is conducted. The pace of change will not slow down. If anything, it will accelerate. But the core challenge organisations face will remain fundamentally human.

They will continue to need help making sense of complexity. They will continue to need challenge, perspective, and support. They will continue to need individuals who can sit alongside leadership teams and help them navigate decisions that carry real consequences. That is the role of the advisor. Not as the holder of answers, but as the enabler of better ones.

Final Reflection

The question, then, is not whether advisors are still needed. It is whether we understand what the role has become. The era of the distant expert delivering pre-packaged solutions is fading. In its place is a more grounded, relational, and adaptive model—one built on trust, shared outcomes, and the willingness to engage deeply with complexity.

As Stephen, Avneet, and Meehir collectively demonstrate, the modern advisor is not defined by what they know alone, but by how they think, how they engage, and how they help organisations move forward. In a world where the answers are easier to find than ever before, that kind of thinking is not redundant. It is essential.
