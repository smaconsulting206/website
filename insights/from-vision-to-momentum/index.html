From Vision to Momentum: A Practitioner-Informed Framework for Strategic Advisory and Organisational Transformation

Companion to The Uncommon Advisor, S1 E3

Dr Stephen Anthony (Institute of Six Sigma Professionals), Meehir Patel, Avneet Singh Riat (Angel Advisors)

1. Introduction

Strategic advisory work occupies a complex and often misunderstood position within contemporary organisational practice. Although widely utilised across sectors, the nature of consulting engagements is frequently opaque to the organisations that rely upon them. Many leaders approach consultants with uncertainty, expressing confusion about what consultants actually do, how engagements unfold, and what value is created through the process. This ambiguity reflects a broader disconnect between the lived practice of consulting and the conceptual frameworks commonly presented in academic literature.

The insights that inform this article derive from experienced practitioners reflecting on their work across public sector, private sector, and SME environments. Their perspectives reveal a nuanced understanding of how organisations articulate vision, confront their current reality, and navigate the complexities of change. These insights provide a rich qualitative foundation for developing a structured, academically grounded model of strategic advisory practice. The article synthesises these practitioner perspectives into a coherent framework comprising eight interdependent phases: articulating vision, diagnosing current reality, conducting gap analysis, managing temporal horizons, constructing strategic roadmaps, navigating local versus international complexity, sustaining momentum, and resourcing transformation.

The aim is to bridge the gap between practitioner experience and academic theory, offering a holistic methodology for organisational transformation that is both conceptually rigorous and grounded in lived practice.

2. Articulating Vision: The Foundational Act of Strategy

Vision formation constitutes the foundational act of strategic engagement. It is not merely an aspirational statement but a communicative and cultural intervention that shapes organisational identity, direction, and momentum. As one practitioner notes, “unless you can articulate it… unless you can bring people along with you, it’s going nowhere.” This observation aligns with Kotter’s (1996) argument that vision must be compelling, communicable, and emotionally resonant if it is to mobilise collective action.

Vision operates fundamentally as a narrative. It is a story about the future that leaders must be able to tell, repeat, and embed within organisational discourse. Strategic narrative theory suggests that organisations mobilise around stories rather than abstract goals. Practitioners frequently assist clients in transforming vague aspirations into coherent narratives that can be communicated across the organisation.

This narrative function is essential for aligning stakeholders and establishing a shared understanding of strategic intent. Vision becomes most powerful when embedded within organisational culture. One practitioner draws on experience in heavy industry to illustrate this point, noting that in the steel sector, health and safety was present in “every meeting you would go to,” forming part of the organisation’s identity. This reflects Schein’s (2010) argument that culture is expressed through habitual practices, rituals, and shared assumptions.

When continuous improvement becomes part of the organisational DNA, it ceases to be a project and becomes a cultural norm. Vision formation is not a singular event but an iterative process. Practitioners describe multiple stages — initial exploration, formal articulation, mid-engagement recalibration, and final consolidation. This iterative model aligns with Mintzberg’s (1994) concept of emergent strategy, which recognises that strategy evolves through interaction, reflection, and adaptation rather than through a single moment of definition.

A recurring challenge in advisory work is distinguishing between what clients want and what they need. Organisations sometimes “over-vision and over-strive,” pursuing fashionable ideas such as artificial intelligence without considering whether these ambitions are realistic or aligned with organisational capability. Drucker (2007) argues that effective strategy requires disciplined focus on organisational strengths and genuine needs rather than on trends or managerial enthusiasm.

3. Diagnosing Current Reality

If vision defines the destination, diagnosis defines the starting point. Understanding the current situation is as important as articulating the future. Practitioners emphasise that clients often misperceive their current reality due to emotional overwhelm, operational noise, or cultural blind spots. Without a clear understanding of where the organisation stands, any roadmap becomes speculative.

Organisations devote disproportionate attention to analysing past performance. One consultant asks, “how many people are worrying about yesterday?” and notes that many leaders spend their time in production meetings, customer complaints meetings, and operational reviews. This backward-looking bias reflects Argyris’s (1991) observation that organisations often become trapped in defensive routines that inhibit learning and forward-looking analysis. Effective diagnosis requires a holistic assessment across multiple dimensions: financial performance, customer positioning, market dynamics, operational capability, technological maturity, cultural health, leadership behaviours, and organisational structure.

These dimensions mirror established diagnostic frameworks such as SWOT analysis and the broader strategic positioning literature (Porter, 1996). Diagnosis must be both structured and contextual, recognising the unique characteristics of each organisation. Diagnosis must acknowledge existing strengths. Practitioners note that clients often feel overwhelmed by what is not working, but consultants must help them recognise “there is actually quite a lot of things you are doing well.”

This approach aligns with Cooperrider and Srivastva’s (1987) appreciative inquiry, which argues that sustainable change is built on existing competencies rather than solely on problem identification. Diagnosis is not purely analytical; it is emotional. Organisations may feel shame, frustration, or defensiveness when confronting their current reality. Consultants must navigate these emotions with sensitivity, ensuring that diagnosis becomes a constructive process rather than a demoralising one.

Emotional intelligence is therefore a critical competency in advisory work. Gap analysis identifies the discrepancy between current state and desired future. It is the bridge between diagnosis and action. Practitioners describe two modes of gap analysis, reflecting differences between corporate and SME environments.

4.Structured Gap Analysis in Corporate Contexts

Corporates often possess abundant data, enabling structured gap analysis using SMART objectives, maturity models, KPI frameworks, and process maps. These tools allow consultants to quantify gaps, prioritise interventions, and construct detailed transformation plans. Structured gap analysis provides analytical clarity and supports evidence-based decision-making (Kaplan & Norton, 2004). In smaller organisations, gap analysis emerges through dialogue, intuition, and market observation.

Practitioners describe engagements where “there was no mapping of anything… it was just conversation.” This conversational mode reflects the reality that SMEs often operate with tacit knowledge rather than formal documentation (Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995). Breaking gaps into themes such as leadership, culture, processes, technology, and values makes large visions cognitively manageable and aligns with modular strategy design. Thematic decomposition enhances clarity and supports phased implementation.

5. Time Horizons and Strategic Temporality

Time is both a constraint and a psychological factor in strategy execution. Practitioners explore how organisations conceptualise time and how these conceptions shape strategic behaviour. Timelines must be held “softly,” recognising that external forces such as market volatility, human emotion, and environmental change can disrupt rigid plans. This flexible temporality aligns with agile strategy methodologies.

Different cultures conceptualise time differently. Hofstede’s (2001) cultural dimensions illustrate how national culture influences organisational expectations around planning, pacing, and responsiveness. Consultants must understand these cultural orientations when designing strategy. The classic project management triangle illustrates how time interacts with cost and quality in non-linear ways.

Organisations must make conscious trade-offs, recognising that accelerating timelines may require additional resources or compromise quality.

6. Constructing the Roadmap

The roadmap transforms vision into actionable structure. It provides direction, reassurance, and a sense of progress. A roadmap is “a series of steps… a journey, not just the destination.” It breaks down complex transformations into manageable phases, reducing psychological tension and anchoring decision-making.

Roadmaps provide cognitive structure and operational clarity. Roadmaps must accommodate detours, obstacles, and emergent opportunities. This adaptive approach aligns with Mintzberg’s (1994) argument that strategy must remain responsive to changing circumstances. The roadmap also functions as a psychological contract between consultant and client.

It sets expectations, defines responsibilities, and creates a shared understanding of the journey ahead. This contract fosters trust and reduces ambiguity.

7. Local vs International Considerations

Practitioners introduce a nuanced discussion of locality, exploring how cultural, operational, and logistical factors shape strategic decisions. Business cultures differ dramatically across regions. Hofstede’s (2001) cultural dimensions illustrate how national culture influences organisational behaviour, communication, and decision-making. Consultants must therefore incorporate cultural analysis into strategic design.

Internationalisation introduces hidden risks such as piracy, time zones, language barriers, and supply chain fragility. These risks align with the literature on global operations management, which emphasises the complexity of cross-border coordination. Rather than “local vs international,” practitioners propose “close vs far” — a psychologically grounded framing that emphasises operational proximity. This lens encourages organisations to consider relational, cultural, and logistical distance when making strategic decisions.

8. Maintaining Momentum

Momentum is described as the lifeblood of transformation. Without momentum, even the most compelling vision and roadmap will falter. A four-quadrant model distinguishes between quick wins, long hauls, motivators, and low-value tasks. Quick wins provide early success; long hauls deliver strategic impact; motivators sustain morale; and low-value tasks are deprioritised.

This model aligns with behavioural insights into motivation and reinforcement. Intangible levers such as shared values, team rituals, recognition practices, and social bonding play a critical role in sustaining momentum. These factors mirror organisational psychology research on cohesion and collective efficacy. Momentum emerges when each step builds naturally on the last.

Growth falters when alignment breaks — when actions no longer connect meaningfully to the vision. Alignment therefore constitutes the structural foundation of momentum (Kaplan & Norton, 2004).

9. Resourcing the Journey

Resourcing is not a final step but a continuous thread woven through all others. It encompasses people, technology, capital, capability, and organisational design. Leaders must allocate time to tomorrow, not yesterday. As one practitioner notes, “managers worry about today and yesterday; leaders worry about today and tomorrow.”

This distinction reflects Drucker’s (2007) argument that leadership requires prioritising long-term direction over short-term operational demands. Resourcing extends beyond people, encompassing automation, technology, capital investment, capability mapping, and organisational design. Effective resourcing requires both strategic foresight and operational discipline. Resourcing can be understood as energy distribution — ensuring the right type of energy is applied to the right part of the roadmap at the right time.

This metaphor captures the fluidity and intentionality required in effective resourcing.

10. Conclusion

This article synthesises practitioner insights into a structured, academically grounded model of strategic advisory practice. The eight-stage framework — vision, current reality, gap analysis, time, roadmap, locality, momentum, and resourcing — offers a holistic, human-centred approach to organisational transformation. It integrates emotional regulation, cultural nuance, operational excellence, and behavioural motivation into a single coherent methodology. The model contributes to applied strategy literature by bridging conceptual theory with grounded practitioner insight.

Future research may test this framework empirically across sectors, cultures, and organisational sizes, exploring how different organisational contexts shape the dynamics of strategic advisory work.
